# About AI

So AI has been coming up. People have been impressed with what it can do.
Some even hope to put us all out of a job and make up the difference in profits...
Personally, I've not been too worried about that.

## Expectations vs Reality

I have the impression that a lot of people expect AI to be a smarter more competent human.
One that makes little to no mistakes.

And then we get disappointed.
There's even a name for that these days.
We get AI slop.
It makes me think of the uncanny valley.

The reality is that AI may be more knowledgeable. But it still has a non-perfect grasp of the knowledge it has. And it, like humans, will make assertions based on that dubious understanding.
It's just that it will, like humans, make them with confidence.

And then someone spots all the mistakes.
Is it fair to begrudge AI those mistakes?
As it turns out AI has a larger short- and long-term memory. In that sense it is better than a human.

But it has neither experience or wisdom.
The mistakes it makes can be very dumb.
Is that a problem though?

## We know how to fix our mistakes.

Anyone who's ever written or used software knows about bugs.
Humans make mistakes.

We also know how to catch our mistakes early.
This has been a hotly debated topic for a while before it died down.
The issue has been settled for a while, though adoption is not there yet.

We fix our human mistakes with a variety of tools and processes:
- Test Driven Development
- Behavior Driven Development or Acceptance Tests Driven Development or Specification by Example
- Static Code Analysis
- Mutation Testing
- Property Testing
- Quality Assurance (not so manual)

### The problem with motivation

I mentioned adoption of the techniques listed above has not fully happened yet.

The problem is humans struggle to find motivation to spend the effort required to apply all of these.
For example, who doesn't roll their eyes at Sonar gates blocking their submissions? I know I do. And then I haven't even tried to talk about Test Driven Development. Most teams still do something called Test After Development instead.

### AI doesn't complain

This project has been built by AI using these techniques.
And interestingly, in a multi agent setup where every agent has a focussed role it allows them to
detect their own mistakes and even fix them.

Is this surprising?
When AI are so similar to humans as fallible entities?

I feel like this is a breakthrough.
The question now is really just the energy and water costs required to run these things.

## How it works in practice

### Swarmforge

I'm using a tool created by Robert and Justin Martin. Also by letting it be generated by AI using the same principles. See: https://github.com/unclebob/swarm-forge

It boots up a number of agents in specific roles and handles the coordination between them.

The following roles are supported:
- Specifier
- Coder
- Cleaner
- Architect
- Hardener
- QA

I used a variant where the cleaner and hardener roles have been divided over a new Refactorer-role and the existing Architect-role. I would ask the Specifier to occasionally perform QA tasks.

For more details on Swarmforge see the project page.

### Preparation

Before starting development I made sure the full rule-set for the game Monopoly would be available in a format for AI to read. This process consisted of asking the AI to...

- Parse the text from a scan of the game instruction manual.
- Analyze an image of the game board to extract the space names and colour groups and their location on the board.
- Cross-reference the streets extracted from the image with detailed information of each street found on the web.
- Extract the Chance- and Community Chest card values from the web.

Next I asked the AI to write up a phased approach to development of the game using the given rules. As well as 1 computer controlled player strategy. A greedy one that would simply try to buy everything if it is able to.

### Specifier

Writes specifications using Gherkin syntax based on the information from the game rule-set and the phased plan for development.

The specifier is the independent party that makes sure the [Coder](#Coder) does not deviate from the plan. The specifier does not make it's own decisions but makes proposals to be approved by a human. A kind of Supervisor.

This is the reason for the Gherkin syntax as it is a well understood human readable language that is formal enough to be converted into executable tests. In principal anyone with sufficient knowledge of the problem domain can validate these specifications. 

AI models intended for routine tasks appear enough for this role.
E.g:

- Claude Sonnet 5
- GPT 5.6-luna
- GPT 5.4-mini
- DeepSeek V4 Flash

### Coder

Executes the aceptance tests (specifications) provided by the [Specifier](#Specifier) and writes the code needed to produce the desired result. The Coder will apply Test Driven Development to write the production code.

AI models intended for routine tasks appear enough for this role.
E.g:

- Claude Sonnet 5
- GPT 5.6-luna
- DeepSeek V4 Flash

### Refactorer

The refactorer analyses the coder written by the [Coder](#Coder) and applies improvements without changing the behavior of the code. Clear naming, complexity reduction, etc...

All tests are executed to prevent introducing defects.

AI models intended for routine tasks appear enough for this role.
E.g:

- Claude Sonnet 5
- GPT 5.6-luna
- GPT 5.4-mini
- DeepSeek V4 Flash

### Architect

The architect analyses the code and applies changes as per desired design patterns. This is done without changing the behaviour of the code. The architect will also look for unspecified behavior and flag this with the [Specifier](#Specifier)

All tests are executed to prevent introducing defects.

Depending on the kind of problem the Architect discovers the appropriate agent is given follow-up work to deal with it. Perhaps the [Coder](#Coder) or [Refactorer](#Refactorer) missed something? 

AI models intended for day-to-day tasks appear enough for this role.
E.g:

- Caude Opus 5
- GPT 5.6-terra

## AI Slop

I already touched upon the concept of AI slop [earlier in this document](#expectations-vs-reality).

So did this experiment yield AI slop?

To be honest? No.

The code may not have been written exactly to the standards I prefer. But the same happens with my colleagues at work. And I'm not one to believe my colleagues produce poor work. We are simply different. With our own strengts and weaknesses.

I primarily care if the code has sufficient test coverage. After that I grant artistic freedom when it comes to the solution. Within limits, of course. I still had a [Refactorer](#Refactorer) and [Architect](#Architect) agent enforce some standards.

The first version of the game as written by the AI agents worked reasonably.
As it turned out there were two main issues with this version:

### Ambiguity

Upon starting the game for the first time the pawns would consistently land on the space forcing them to pay $200 in taxes and instantly bankrupt. But players are supposed to have a starting capital of  $1500 so how could this be?

There were acceptance tests asserting the starting capital.
And they were passing.

The problem was ambiguity behind the question to start the game from the CLI.

Understand that AI models are trained on human knowledge.
And like humans, this existing knowledge informs our expectations when confronted with ambiguity.

In this case the AI assumed a CLI command should complete quickly. A fair assumption a human could also have made. Surely most commands do in fact produce a result quickly.

But commands that require time to produce a result also exist. And in the case of a game of Monopoly, especially with 8 players, it could take some time.

Us humans have experience and wisdom, so we understand the overlap between the game of Monopoly and a videogame. Thus, we know that in this case the command can't always end quickly.

AI does not understand and as a result will do anything to meet the requirement born from it's own assumption. Including... cheating.

In this case by forcing players to immediately pay taxes and start with a starting capital insufficient to do so.

After a first attempt to force the AI to give players their full $1500 starting capital. The AI cheated again by giving one of the players a street with prebuilt houses forcing the opponent to pay an excess of $1500 in rent.

That is what the [Coder](#Coder) did at least.
The [Architect](#Architect) considered it troublesome the [Specifier](#Specifier) had mandated the starting capital be $1500. Because it made it difficult for the [Coder](#Coder) to end the game quickly. This was, according to him, in conflict with the requirement the CLI give a result quickly.

This was the first instance the AI informed me it had made up this requirement. A clarification that the CLI was to play to completion and thus be potentially long-running, allowed the game to start normally to its conclusion.

### Under-Specification

In principle ambiguity is a variant of under-specification. But what I mean here are things I simply forgot. I'm only human and can't think of everything ahead of time.

The plan looked ok. I couldn't come up with extra tasks.
And there's no need. I can observe the result and act on that feedback.

So at my second attempt to run the simulation, it was played by two players until one fo them won. 

A few things were notable:

- Streets were bought, but building usually took a longer time.
  - Building is only possible once a player owns a full colour group as per the rules.
- The first player sent to jail would immediately move another turn but still believe they're in jail having already left jail.
  - A player landing on "Go to jail" with a double equal value dice roll was still allowed their second turn.
- Streets are occasionally auctioned off to settle debt.
- The impact on player's bank balances was never visualised.
  - Making it difficult to understand why certain things happen.
- No notifications were shown of Chance or Community Chest cards being pulled.
  - The game was actually loaded with empty decks

As expected, specifications were missing both I and the AI did not think of.
So we rinse & repeat.
